    "summary_long": "This source proposes a theory that treats consciousness as a fundamental property of systems capable of integrating information. Tononi begins from two phenomenological observations: conscious experience is highly differentiated, because any one experience rules out a vast repertoire of alternatives, and it is integrated, because each experience is unified and cannot be decomposed into independently experienced fragments. The paper argues that ordinary information measures are insufficient because they count state repertoires without asking whether the states belong to a single causally integrated system. A camera sensor can have an enormous number of possible states while still being only a collection of independent photodiodes; a conscious brain, by contrast, depends on causal interactions among its elements. The proposed measure, Φ, is the amount of effective information available across a complex's minimum information bipartition. A complex is not simply an anatomical region; it is a subset whose elements can integrate information and that is not contained in a larger subset with higher Φ. This lets the theory define both quantity and boundaries of consciousness. The source then extends the framework from quantity to quality: the kind of consciousness a complex has depends on the effective-information matrix among its elements, which defines a relational qualia space. Current activity values of the interaction-mediating variables specify the particular experience at a given moment. The application to neuroscience is central. The thalamocortical system is treated as a plausible substrate because it combines functional specialization with broad reciprocal integration. By contrast, the cerebellum, sensory afferents, motor outputs, subcortical modulatory systems, and cortico-subcortical loops can be powerful or sophisticated without directly joining the main conscious complex when they remain modular, feed-forward, or low-Φ. Sleep and anesthesia show that anatomy is not enough: physiological readiness and differentiated responses are also required. The source's practical interpretation is that consciousness can in principle be graded, measured, and assessed in animals, infants, artifacts, and non-reporting patients, but such measurement depends on difficult approximations of causal integration rather than surface behavior alone.",
