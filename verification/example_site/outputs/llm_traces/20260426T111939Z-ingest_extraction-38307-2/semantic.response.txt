    "summary_long": "This review develops a suppression-centered interpretation of alpha-band oscillations in selective attention. Rather than treating alpha simply as a relaxed or idle rhythm, Foxe and Snyder frame 8-14 Hz activity as a mechanism that can be strategically increased in cortical systems that would otherwise process distracting input. The argument begins from cuing paradigms: when a symbolic cue instructs a participant which modality, location, or feature will matter, the interval before the target reveals how the brain prepares for selection. In that interval, alpha power repeatedly rises over regions corresponding to irrelevant information. In intersensory attention, alpha increases over parieto-occipital cortex when audition is attended and vision must be suppressed. In visual spatial attention, alpha rises over occipital cortex representing the uncued hemifield, and the effect follows retinotopic expectations. In feature-based attention, alpha-reactive sources separate by visual stream, rising in areas associated with the feature dimension that is currently irrelevant. The review also extends the pattern to auditory and tactile attention, where ipsilateral alpha increases suggest sensory-specific suppression outside the visual system. The strongest conceptual step is the distinction between passive idling and active suppression. The Kelly et al. bilateral stimulation design is especially important because alpha rose above pre-cue baseline while visual streams remained continuously active, weakening the idea that alpha merely rebounds after stimulation. TMS studies add causal plausibility: externally driving 10 Hz activity impaired contralateral detection while neighboring frequencies did not. The authors remain cautious about mechanism, separating attention-related alpha from arousal-related alpha and noting possible thalamo-cortical contributions. Still, they conclude that frontal and parietal cortico-cortical control networks are likely central for attention-sensitive alpha modulation. The source is therefore best read as a review-backed model of selective attention in which suppression is an active computational operation, implemented partly through alpha-band synchronization in task-irrelevant sensory circuits.",
