    "summary_long": "Mind the Hype evaluates mindfulness and meditation research at a moment when the topic had moved from a fringe scientific interest into psychotherapy, education, workplaces, military resilience programs, and mass media. The authors' central concern is that the public, clinicians, journalists, and some researchers often treat mindfulness as a single well-defined object, even though the term covers many practices, states, traits, interventions, and measurement instruments. This semantic breadth makes it easy to overgeneralize from a questionnaire score, a short app-based exercise, a structured MBSR course, or a long retreat to mindfulness as such. The article recognizes selective promise, especially around MBCT for depressive relapse and some symptoms such as anxiety, depression, and pain, but it rejects the idea that the clinical evidence is broadly settled. Much research remains early-stage, relies on wait-list or treatment-as-usual comparisons, and includes little ordinary-practice evidence. Interventions labeled mindfulness-based differ in content, duration, delivery mode, instructor training, homework, and target population, so broad efficacy claims can mask major differences in dose and mechanism.\n\nA major contribution is the paper's treatment of safety. It challenges assumptions that meditation has minimal adverse effects simply because harms have not been rigorously studied. Case reports and observational studies describe serious or distressing experiences, including psychosis, mania, depersonalization, anxiety, panic, traumatic-memory reexperiencing, and deterioration.
