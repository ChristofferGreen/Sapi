    "summary_long": "John Preskill's essay defines a practical turning point in quantum computing: the NISQ era, where devices become large enough to stress brute-force classical simulation but remain too noisy for fully reliable, fault-tolerant computation. The central argument is deliberately balanced. Quantum computing has deep reasons to matter: known algorithms, complexity-theory evidence, and the persistent inability of classical machines to simulate general quantum evolution all point toward genuine super-classical capabilities. Yet the paper repeatedly separates long-term confidence from near-term deliverability. NISQ systems may have 50 to a few hundred qubits, but two-qubit gate errors, measurement errors, connectivity, fabrication yield, and circuit depth all determine what they can actually do. Preskill therefore treats near-term devices less as universal commercial disruptors than as experimental platforms for discovering algorithms, validating heuristics, and probing many-body physics. Hybrid algorithms such as QAOA and VQE are worth trying, especially because hardware availability can accelerate empirical algorithm development, but the paper does not claim they will beat mature classical methods. The strongest near-term scientific case is quantum simulation of highly entangled dynamics, where classical computers are structurally weak and devices of order one hundred qubits may already reveal new phenomena. The long-term transformative applications, including large-scale factoring and accurate simulation of complex chemistry and materials, are tied to fault tolerance. Because useful protected computations may require millions of physical qubits, the paper urges sustained work on lowering gate error rates and developing error correction while keeping expectations for NISQ grounded.",
