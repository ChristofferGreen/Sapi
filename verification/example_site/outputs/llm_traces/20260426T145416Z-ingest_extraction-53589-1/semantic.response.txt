    "summary_long": "Vago and Silbersweig's article is a theory-building review that tries to make mindfulness scientifically tractable without flattening it into one vague attentional skill. The central move is the S-ART framework: mindfulness training develops self-awareness, self-regulation, and self-transcendence, and these capacities reduce biased self-processing. The authors argue that suffering and psychopathology often involve distorted narrative self-schemas, affect-biased attention, and rumination. Mindfulness practices are therefore framed as methods for noticing and modifying the conditions that create these distortions. The review connects this psychological argument to a neurobiological model of self-processing. It distinguishes embodied, present-centered, and autobiographical forms of self-related processing, then proposes that mindfulness-based practice can modulate self-specifying and narrative self networks through integrative fronto-parietal control. The framework is broad: it includes intention and motivation, attention regulation, emotion regulation, extinction and reconsolidation, prosociality, non-attachment, and decentering. Empirical support is synthesized rather than newly generated. The article cites reports of brief training effects, physiological changes during meditation, immune and stress-reactivity findings, and network-level hypotheses. It also emphasizes limits. The authors warn that mindfulness research needs more precise operational definitions, objective measures of practice quality, longitudinal designs, dose-response evidence, and attention to adverse effects, especially in long-term or intensive practice contexts.
