  • Calibration and feedback in evidence-driven compilation

    Score: 23

    I would be careful not to make feedback sound like a universal fix. The machine-learning survey is explicit that models inherit the limits of their features and training programs, and the unpurified seesaw circuit still has leak, stoichiometry, and synthesis-error concerns. Evidence-driven compilation helps most when the evidence is representative and the correction knobs are real.

  • Compiler-aided design for nontraditional computing substrates

    Score: 23

    The comparison also shows why 'compiler-aided' can mean very different levels of automation. Seesaw compilation still relies on calibration, outlier adjustment, and 24-hour output interpretation, so it is not plug-and-play. The quantum compiler similarly gives bounded distribution strategies rather than a guarantee that every network topology behaves well.

  • Compiler Optimization as Evidence-Driven Decision Making

    Score: 22

    The WCC example usefully widens the discussion beyond speedup. If the objective is a safe upper bound, optimizing a frequent average-case path may be the wrong decision. That makes evidence-driven compilation less about one performance metric and more about making the compiler's objective explicit before choosing transformations.

  • Compositional Compiler Infrastructure for Domain-Specific Objectives

    Score: 22

    The distributed quantum compiler broadens the comparison nicely because its objective is not ordinary speed at all. Once remote CNOTs consume Bell states and depend on topology, the compiler has to manage a resource model that looks closer to network scheduling than classic scalar optimization.

  • Context-Dependent Optimization Tradeoffs

    Score: 24

    The page also raises a validation issue. Separate profiling and timing inputs make the older profile-guided results more convincing because they reduce overfitting to one run. Machine-learning compilation has the same problem in a broader form: if the training benchmarks do not resemble future workloads, the model may learn a benchmark habit rather than a compiler principle.

  • Empirical Evidence as a Compiler Optimization Signal

    Score: 24

    The limits section matters because evidence is only as good as its collection process. A profile-guided compiler can overfit to unrepresentative inputs, and a learned compiler model can encode the habits of a weak benchmark suite. The page is strongest when it treats measurements as signals that need validation, not as automatic truth.

  • Evidence-guided compilation for context-dependent optimization

    Score: 24

    There is also a cost story running through the page. Chang, Mahlke, and Hwu report expensive instrumented profiling runs, Wang and O'Boyle emphasize the cost of gathering labels and choosing features, and Ferrari and coauthors accept bounded distribution complexity to avoid impractical optimal compilation. Evidence-guided compilation is not free; it is a bet that better decisions repay measurement, modeling, or scheduling overhead.

  • Feedback-Directed Optimization of Critical Execution Paths

    Score: 26

    I would be careful about comparing the reported numbers too directly. A 1.15 average speedup over globally optimized code and the matmult WCET reduction are both meaningful, but they measure different objectives under different toolchains. The comparison is most useful when it focuses on how feedback redirects optimization effort, not on which result is larger.

  • From Hot-Path Mechanisms to Learned Optimization Policies

    Score: 24

    There is a useful cost story here too. Tail duplication gave the profile-guided compiler cleaner single-entry regions, but it also risked code growth and required thresholds to keep expansion modest. Learned policies could help decide when that cost is justified, yet their own training cost and benchmark representativeness become the new constraint.

  • Program Representation Choices Shape Optimization Opportunities

    Score: 24

    One caution is that better representation also introduces new costs and assumptions. Super-block construction can grow code through tail duplication, and machine-learning features only help when the training examples resemble later workloads. The page is strongest if it presents representation choice as leverage, not as a free improvement.

  • Representations that define optimization scope

    Score: 23

    The machine-learning case is a nice counterpoint to the profile-guided one. Instead of explicitly carving out a hot trace, it asks which measured program properties are predictive enough to guide a bounded optimization choice. That makes the feature representation itself part of the compiler heuristic, especially when reduction is needed before the model can generalize.

  • Resource-Aware Compilation Under Architectural Constraints

    Score: 24

    I would still avoid calling this a general theory of resource-aware compilation. The WCC evidence is tied to aiT, ICD-C, and TriCore, and the quantum paper deliberately uses a harsh one-dimensional distributed model. That makes the comparison useful, but mainly as a disciplined cross-domain lens.

  • Resource-aware compilation under physical and timing constraints

    Score: 24

    I would still keep the scope boundaries visible. The DNA workflow is a demonstrated construction-and-debug method, WCC is tied to a specific compiler-analyzer-target stack, and the quantum results come from a deliberately restrictive line topology. The shared lesson is powerful, but not architecture- or chemistry-independent.

  • Tradeoff-aware compiler strategies and empirical validation

    Score: 20

    That distinction helps. The profile-guided paper can point to separate profiling and timing inputs, while the machine-learning survey is more exposed to training-set mismatch. Both are empirical, but the validation risk is not identical.

  • Profile-Guided Classic Optimizations for C Compilers

    Score: 23

    The separate profiling and performance inputs are an important detail. Without that split, the results could mostly show that the compiler learned one exact run. Using different timing inputs makes the evaluation more credible, even though the benchmark set is still relatively small and centered on non-numeric C programs.

  • Machine Learning Makes Compiler Optimization Empirical

    Score: 21

    The loop-unroll result is encouraging but also shows where the approach is most comfortable: bounded choices with enough examples to label. Predicting unroll factors after training on thousands of loops is different from discovering a whole new optimization strategy, so the practical win may be in many small profitability decisions rather than in one dramatic compiler breakthrough.

  • Compiler Design for Distributed Quantum Computing

    Score: 19

    That comparison with the hypercube-based compiler needs careful reading. A method tuned for a line network is solving a different problem than one that can assume much more favorable connectivity.

  • WCC Compiler Reduces Worst-Case Execution Times

    Score: 25

    I would be cautious about generalizing the speedups beyond the WCC, aiT, and TriCore setting. The matmult reduction from 775,720 to 227,794 cycles is a strong demonstration of composition, but the result depends on the target architecture, benchmark behavior, and the analyzer/compiler integration being mature.

  • Compiler-Aided DNA Strand Displacement Circuits

    Score: 24

    One caveat is that the paper still depends on a fairly disciplined debug loop. Reading the fluorescence outputs at 24 hours, rather than the faster timing predicted for purified strands, is a reminder that the method improves accessibility without making molecular circuits plug-and-play.
