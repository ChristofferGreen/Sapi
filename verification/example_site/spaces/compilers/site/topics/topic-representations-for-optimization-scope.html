Representations that define optimization scope

Structure: wiki

Representation choices

Super-blocks are linear basic-block sequences with a single entry and multiple exits, formed by trace selection and tail duplication to expose hot-path transformations. Compiler-learning systems use quantifiable program features, but high-dimensional feature spaces often require pruning or projection before models can generalize. Distributed quantum compilers model worst-case and topology-dependent connectivity so remote CNOTs, entanglement swapping, and data-qubit placement can be reasoned about explicitly.

Shared concept

Across the sources, the representation chosen by the compiler determines which optimization opportunities, dependencies, and constraints become visible. Profile-guided classic optimization turns hot traces into super-blocks. Machine-learning optimization depends on feature representations and dimension reduction. Distributed quantum compilation represents a networked quantum machine through processors, communication qubits, quantum links, topology constraints, and remote operations.

Synthesis value

A synthesis page can treat super-blocks, feature vectors, and quantum coupling maps as examples of compiler representations that are not neutral: each representation decides what the optimizer can see, what costs it can estimate, and what transformations or mappings it can legally consider.

  • Feature reduction preserves signal

    Feature selection and dimension reduction are necessary in compiler-learning problems because larger feature spaces require more training examples and can contain redundant information.

  • Open questions extend beyond optimization

    Future research in machine-learning compilation extends beyond choosing optimizations to mapping program and hardware spaces, learning analyses, discovering common features, and possibly learning better intermediate...

  • Training data limits learning

    Machine-learning-based compiler optimization is limited by the quality, representativeness, and cost of its training data and cannot by itself create legal transformations or derive legality analyses.

  • Super-blocks isolate hot paths

    Super-blocks represent frequently executed paths as linear basic-block sequences that can only be entered at the first block but may exit from any block.

  • Duplication exposes producer relationships

    Tail duplication creates additional optimization opportunities because it can make an earlier operation the sole producer of operands used later in a hot path.

  • Data swapping lowers asymptotic overhead

    The data-qubit-swapping-based strategy arranges data qubits so each CNOT in a layer operates on neighboring processors, and its compiled per-layer depth does not exceed n/4 dqs plus dqs prime.

  • Worst case maximizes overhead

    The paper's worst-case system model uses one data qubit per processor and a one-dimensional nearest-neighbor processor topology, making every CNOT remote and maximizing distributed-computation overhead.

  • Tail duplication forms super-blocks

    Trace selection followed by tail duplication converts frequently executed traces into super-blocks by removing control-flow entries into the middle of a trace.

The page makes a useful cross-cutting point: representations are where a compiler decides what can be optimized at all. A weighted flow graph makes hot paths visible, feature vectors make model training possible, and a quantum topology model makes remote-gate costs impossible to ignore. Those are very different domains, but the abstraction boundary is doing similar work in each one.

Eli Okafor

I would put a little more stress on the non-neutrality of these choices. Super-blocks privilege frequent traces, PCA-reduced feature sets privilege measurable properties, and a one-dimensional quantum network privileges worst-case routing constraints. Each representation clarifies one part of the problem while hiding or compressing something else.

The super-block example is a good anchor because it shows representation changing both profitability and legality conditions. That helps explain why the later machine-learning and quantum examples should also be read as compiler-design choices, not just implementation details.
