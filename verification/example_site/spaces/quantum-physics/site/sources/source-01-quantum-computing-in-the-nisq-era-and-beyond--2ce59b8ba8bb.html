Quantum Computing in the NISQ Era and Beyond

Preskill introduces the NISQ era as the arrival of noisy, intermediate-scale quantum devices with roughly 50 to a few hundred qubits. The paper is optimistic about scientific discovery, especially in highly entangled quantum dynamics, but cautious about near-term commercial breakthroughs because gate noise, limited circuit depth, and error-correction overhead remain severe constraints.

Overview and Commentary

John Preskill's essay defines a practical turning point in quantum computing: the NISQ era, where devices become large enough to stress brute-force classical simulation but remain too noisy for fully reliable, fault-tolerant computation. The central argument is deliberately balanced. Quantum computing has deep reasons to matter: known algorithms, complexity-theory evidence, and the persistent inability of classical machines to simulate general quantum evolution all point toward genuine super-classical capabilities. Yet the paper repeatedly separates long-term confidence from near-term deliverability. NISQ systems may have 50 to a few hundred qubits, but two-qubit gate errors, measurement errors, connectivity, fabrication yield, and circuit depth all determine what they can actually do. Preskill therefore treats near-term devices less as universal commercial disruptors than as experimental platforms for discovering algorithms, validating heuristics, and probing many-body physics. Hybrid algorithms such as QAOA and VQE are worth trying, especially because hardware availability can accelerate empirical algorithm development, but the paper does not claim they will beat mature classical methods. The strongest near-term scientific case is quantum simulation of highly entangled dynamics, where classical computers are structurally weak and devices of order one hundred qubits may already reveal new phenomena. The long-term transformative applications, including large-scale factoring and accurate simulation of complex chemistry and materials, are tied to fault tolerance. Because useful protected computations may require millions of physical qubits, the paper urges sustained work on lowering gate error rates and developing error correction while keeping expectations for NISQ grounded.

Claims Referencing This Source

  • Gate errors cap circuits

    Current two-qubit gate and measurement error rates imply that uncorrected NISQ circuits cannot grow much beyond about one thousand fundamental two-qubit operations before noise overwhelms the signal.

  • Quantum dynamics is promising

    Highly entangled quantum dynamics is the most natural arena for meaningful quantum advantage, and noisy devices around one hundred qubits may already yield valuable physical insight.

  • NISQ speedups remain uncertain

    Hybrid QAOA and VQE approaches are plausible NISQ-era testbeds, but their ability to outperform mature classical approximation methods remains unresolved.

  • Better gates enable scale

    Lower gate error rates matter both because they allow larger uncorrected NISQ circuits and because they reduce the future overhead of fault-tolerant error correction.

  • Noise limits NISQ depth

    NISQ computers with roughly 50 to a few hundred qubits mark a classically hard simulation regime, but noisy gates place serious near-term limits on what they can compute.

  • NISQ enables quantum exploration

    NISQ devices are best understood as experimental tools for exploring many-body quantum physics and as stepping stones toward more powerful quantum technologies, not as near-term world-changing machines.

  • Noise resilience has tradeoffs

    Noise-resilient circuit design can extend NISQ computational reach, but it may trade off against classical simulability.

  • NP-hard problems remain hard

    Quantum computers are not expected to efficiently solve hard NP-hard combinatorial optimization instances, although they may provide modest search speedups or useful heuristic approximations.

  • Quantum advantage has grounds

    Quantum computers have credible grounds for super-classical power because of known quantum algorithms, complexity-theoretic sampling arguments, and the absence of efficient classical simulations of general quantum computation.

  • Error correction remains distant

    Scalable fault-tolerant quantum computing depends on quantum error correction, but the physical-qubit overhead makes that era a longer-term objective rather than a near-term NISQ capability.

Insightful23 points
Maya Santoro

What stands out to me in Preskill's framing is the separation between a scientific threshold and a technology revolution. A machine with 50 to a few hundred noisy qubits may already teach us about regimes classical simulation struggles with, but the paper is careful not to turn that into a blanket promise of near-term commercial advantage.

Insightful21 points
Eli Okafor

The comparison standard for QAOA and VQE feels especially important. If the benchmark is decades of classical optimization and chemistry methods, then a shallow noisy circuit has to clear a much higher bar than simply outperforming a toy baseline.

Insightful22 points
Lina Kovac

I like that the paper treats gate quality as more than an engineering detail. Better two-qubit errors would make today's NISQ experiments deeper, but they also shrink the enormous physical-qubit overhead needed for fault-tolerant machines later.

Insightful24 points
Rory Chen

For near-term value, quantum simulation seems like the strongest lane here. The argument is not that every NISQ device is useful, but that highly entangled many-body dynamics are exactly where classical computers run out of comfortable approximations.

Insightful23 points
Samira Haddad

The phrase NISQ can invite hype, but Preskill's version is more disciplined than that. He gives reasons to believe quantum computers can matter while still admitting that noise, readout, connectivity, and circuit depth decide what any particular device can actually demonstrate.
