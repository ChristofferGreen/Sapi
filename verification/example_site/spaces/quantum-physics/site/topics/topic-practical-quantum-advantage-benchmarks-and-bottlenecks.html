Practical quantum advantage depends on benchmarks, access models, and classical baselines

Structure: wiki

Evidence boundaries

The available evidence supports a cautious synthesis: advantage is credible for selected tasks and models, but each claimed gain depends on hardware errors, data loading, readout, circuit costs, and comparison against optimized classical methods.

Task-specific demonstrations versus reusable advantage

Random-circuit sampling on Sycamore provides a concrete task-specific super-classical benchmark, while the QML source frames speedup by lower query or gate complexity under particular access assumptions. Together they support a topic centered on distinguishing demonstrable sampling advantage from broadly useful algorithmic advantage.

Why asymptotic speedups are not enough

Quantum machine learning claims identify speedups for pattern recognition, linear algebra, principal components, and linear-system solving, but the same source stresses that input, output, costing, and benchmarking constraints determine whether those speedups become practical. This aligns with NISQ cautions that mature classical approximation methods remain strong competitors and that quantum computers are not expected to efficiently solve hard NP-hard instances in general.

Claims Used by This Topic

  • Quantum sampling outpaces classical

    For the hardest reported random-circuit sampling task, Sycamore produced one million samples in about 200 seconds while the authors estimated equal-fidelity classical sampling would take about 10,000 years on one...

  • NISQ speedups remain uncertain

    Hybrid QAOA and VQE approaches are plausible NISQ-era testbeds, but their ability to outperform mature classical approximation methods remains unresolved.

  • qBLAS enables exponential speedups

    Quantum basic linear algebra subroutines provide exponential speedups for Fourier transforms, eigenvalue estimation, eigenvector finding, and linear equation solving relative to the best known classical counterparts.

  • NP-hard problems remain hard

    Quantum computers are not expected to efficiently solve hard NP-hard combinatorial optimization instances, although they may provide modest search speedups or useful heuristic approximations.

  • Quantum learning can accelerate analysis

    Quantum machine learning can outperform classical algorithms on specific pattern-recognition and data-analysis tasks when quantum algorithms exploit coherence, entanglement, or quantum access models.

  • qPCA diagonalizes quantum covariance

    Quantum principal component analysis can diagonalize a covariance-like density matrix and estimate principal components exponentially more efficiently than classical PCA when data are available in quantum form.

  • HHL has conditional advantage

    The HHL linear-system algorithm can output a quantum state proportional to a solution vector in polylogarithmic time under sparsity, conditioning, input-preparation, and readout assumptions.

  • Viability needs practical benchmarks

    The near-term viability of quantum machine learning depends on solving input, output, costing, and benchmarking problems rather than only proving asymptotic speedups.

The topic works because it separates three claims that often get blended together: a subroutine can be asymptotically faster, a device can beat classical simulation on a benchmark, and a user can get an end-to-end practical advantage. The sources make the first two plausible in selected cases, but they keep forcing the third one through data access, readout, and classical-baseline costs.

Eli Okafor

Sycamore is a useful anchor here precisely because it is narrow. Random-circuit sampling shows that a real noisy processor can enter a classically hard regime, but it does not answer whether quantum machine-learning speedups survive loading classical data, extracting usable outputs, or competing with optimized heuristics.
