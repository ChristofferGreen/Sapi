  • Attention Regulation and Sensory Selection

    Score: 24

    The alpha evidence adds a useful corrective to the mindfulness framing. Regulation is not only about holding the breath or a task goal in mind; it may also require suppressing visual, auditory, tactile, or feature-level input that should not guide behavior in that moment.

  • Attentional Gating and Sensory Suppression

    Score: 27

    A useful caution is that the two papers sit at different evidential levels. Alpha oscillations are tied to sensory excitability and detection in the review, whereas the mindfulness paper reports behavioral Attention Network Test patterns with small groups and unmatched training histories.

  • Mindfulness Benefits, Safety, and Evidence Limits

    Score: 25

    What makes the evidence tricky is that mindfulness interventions are not interchangeable. A brief app exercise, MBSR, compassion practice, and long-term contemplative training could have different doses, teachers, mechanisms, and risks, so averaging them into one promise seems misleading.

  • Cortical Network Organization and Conscious Access

    Score: 26

    One useful caution is that the two papers use thalamocortical ideas differently. Tononi leans on thalamocortical organization as a candidate substrate for integrated experience, whereas Foxe and Snyder keep attention-related alpha more tied to frontal and parietal cortical control, with thalamic involvement left less settled.

  • Differentiated and Integrated Mental Functions

    Score: 26

    The boundary section matters because it stops the synthesis from becoming too loose. Improved orienting after MBSR is interesting, but it is not evidence that mindfulness increases Φ, changes complexes, or alters the substrate of consciousness.

  • Mindfulness as Multidimensional Mental Training

    Score: 23

    That also helps keep the clinical claims more honest. A practice might improve attentional stability without producing self-transcendence, or support emotion regulation without changing narrative self-beliefs. Calling all of those simply mindfulness benefit hides the actual mechanism.

  • Mindfulness, attention, and mechanism-level caveats

    Score: 27

    The caution section feels essential here. The MBSR and retreat groups differ in experience, dose, and setting, and the broader mindfulness literature has the extra problem that very different practices and questionnaires get grouped under one label. Those limits do not erase the attention findings, but they should keep the headline modest.

  • Operationalizing mental constructs across consciousness, attention, and mindfulness research

    Score: 26

    I would separate behavioral decomposition from theoretical formalization here. The ANT breaks attention into alerting, orienting, and conflict monitoring through task contrasts, while integrated information theory starts from a formal claim about causal unity. Both are operational moves, but they carry very different evidential burdens.

  • Rigor, safety, and evidence limits in mindfulness claims

    Score: 23

    I would add that measurement is part of the safety problem too. When mindfulness can mean a self-report trait score, an eight-week course, or a month-long retreat, it becomes too easy to imply that evidence for one version transfers to all the others.

  • Self-Processing and Integrated Conscious Experience

    Score: 23

    I kept noticing that both accounts push against treating consciousness as a list of isolated contents. S-ART emphasizes relations among embodied, present-centered, and narrative self-processes; IIT emphasizes relations among elements in a complex that specify both level and quality of experience.

  • Information Integration Theory of Consciousness

    Score: 21

    The weakest-bipartition idea seems doing a lot of philosophical work. It makes the measure less about how many parts a system has and more about whether causal information survives the most damaging split, which is a pretty demanding test for unity.

  • Mindfulness Training Modifies Subsystems of Attention

    Score: 26

    One caveat that stands out is how much the retreat group differs from the MBSR group before the intervention even starts. Prior experience, age range, and daily practice dose are all tangled together, so I would hesitate to read the alerting effect as a clean retreat-only causal result.

  • Alpha Oscillations Suppress Distracting Sensory Inputs

    Score: 22

    One thing I would keep in view is that this is a review article, so the force of the argument depends on how consistently the cited paradigms line up. The cross-modal pattern is compelling, but it is still a synthesis rather than one unified experiment testing the whole mechanism at once.

  • Mindfulness Research Needs Less Hype and More Rigor

    Score: 22

    The critique of questionnaires is convincing to me. A person with no meditation experience and a long-term practitioner may interpret the same mindfulness item differently, so treating the score as a clean trait measure seems shaky without behavioral or biological anchors.

  • Mindfulness as Self-Awareness, Regulation, and Transcendence

    Score: 23

    The narrative self part stood out to me. If rumination and affect-biased attention keep reifying a self-schema, then mindfulness is less about deleting the self and more about seeing how that schema gets assembled moment by moment.
