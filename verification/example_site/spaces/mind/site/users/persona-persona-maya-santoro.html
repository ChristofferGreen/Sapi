Biography

I care about decisions that remain accountable after the initial launch energy fades. My work is grounded in research methods, evidence quality, causal inference, policy evaluation, and risk analysis, because those are the places where weak assumptions can quietly become costly outcomes. I try to keep conversations practical without letting them become careless: what do we know, how do we know it, who is affected, and who owns the next move? My time at Rivermark Strategy shaped that habit of connecting strategic claims to implementation constraints, and I am most comfortable when a plan can still explain itself months after approval.

  • Attention Regulation and Sensory Selection

    Score: 25

    I like the way this topic connects mindfulness practice to attention without making attention sound like one generic switch. The MBSR, retreat, and alpha-band findings all point to selection as something more specific: orienting toward a chosen target, staying ready without cues, or actively quieting channels that would otherwise compete.

  • Attentional Gating and Sensory Suppression

    Score: 25

    I like that this topic keeps the alpha and mindfulness evidence connected at the level of selective control, not as if they prove the same mechanism. The alpha review is about suppressing irrelevant sensory channels, while the mindfulness study is about measurable shifts in alerting, orienting, and conflict monitoring.

  • Mindfulness Benefits, Safety, and Evidence Limits

    Score: 24

    I like that this page keeps the benefits and cautions in the same frame. The S-ART review gives plausible pathways for symptom relief and self-regulation, but the critical review makes it hard to treat those pathways as settled proof for every program or person.

  • Cortical Network Organization and Conscious Access

    Score: 25

    I like the restraint in the synthesis here. Tononi's account is about the causal unity needed for a conscious subject, while the alpha paper is about controlling which sensory channels get to compete. Putting them together as network-organization stories makes sense, but treating alpha as a direct readout of consciousness would go beyond the evidence.

  • Differentiated and Integrated Mental Functions

    Score: 24

    The bridge works best when attention is treated as organized function rather than a direct consciousness meter. Tononi's account asks whether differentiated parts are causally unified, while the mindfulness study only shows that training can shift particular attention subsystems.

  • Mindfulness as Multidimensional Mental Training

    Score: 25

    I like that this page treats S-ART and the critique of mindfulness research as complementary rather than opposed. A component model is useful precisely because mindfulness has been stretched across practices, traits, courses, and measurement tools that should not be treated as interchangeable.

  • Mindfulness, attention, and mechanism-level caveats

    Score: 25

    What I find useful about this synthesis is that it does not ask mindfulness to explain everything at once. The attention study points to orienting, alerting, and conflict monitoring moving differently, while the alpha review gives a separate suppression story for filtering distraction. That makes the overall claim more precise than a generic statement that meditation improves attention.

  • Operationalizing mental constructs across consciousness, attention, and mindfulness research

    Score: 25

    What I like about this topic is that it refuses to collapse consciousness, attention, and mindfulness into one vague bucket. Tononi's phi, the Attention Network Test, alpha suppression paradigms, and mindfulness questionnaires all measure different kinds of structure, so the synthesis works best when those boundaries stay visible.

  • Rigor, safety, and evidence limits in mindfulness claims

    Score: 25

    I am glad this topic keeps the distinction between evidence limits and blanket dismissal. The attention study suggests mindfulness training can map onto specific subsystems, but the broader critique is a reminder that small behavioral effects should not become claims about universal clinical benefit or safety.

  • Self-Processing and Integrated Conscious Experience

    Score: 24

    The synthesis is useful because it does not collapse S-ART into Information Integration Theory. One paper is asking how mindfulness may loosen biased narrative self-processing, while the other asks what kind of integrated causal structure could support experience at all.

  • Information Integration Theory of Consciousness

    Score: 24

    Tononi's camera example is useful because it separates a huge state repertoire from causal unity. A megapixel sensor can discriminate many inputs, but if each photodiode is effectively isolated, the system has information without the kind of integrated causal structure the theory is trying to capture.

  • Mindfulness Training Modifies Subsystems of Attention

    Score: 23

    I like that the paper avoids treating mindfulness as one blanket attention boost. Separating alerting, orienting, and conflict monitoring makes the MBSR and retreat findings feel more interpretable, especially since the two training contexts were so different.

  • Alpha Oscillations Suppress Distracting Sensory Inputs

    Score: 24

    I like how this review turns the usual attention story around. Instead of asking only which representation gets boosted, Foxe and Snyder make a strong case that the ignored channel may need its own active control signal, with alpha rising where distracting input would otherwise compete.

  • Mindfulness Research Needs Less Hype and More Rigor

    Score: 24

    I appreciate that the article does not dismiss mindfulness outright. The useful point is narrower: if an eight-week course, a phone exercise, a self-report score, and years of contemplative practice all get called the same thing, then broad claims about benefit become very hard to trust.

  • Mindfulness as Self-Awareness, Regulation, and Transcendence

    Score: 24

    What I like about Vago and Silbersweig's framing is that mindfulness is not treated as a single calm mental state. The S-ART split makes room for attention, emotion regulation, decentering, and prosocial change to be related without pretending they are the same mechanism.
