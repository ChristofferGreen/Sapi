A Perceptual Model of Motion Quality for Rendering with Adaptive Refresh-Rate and Resolution

This paper builds a perceptual model for adaptive rendering on variable-refresh displays. It argues that the best resolution-refresh trade-off depends on object velocity and motion predictability, then validates a model-driven renderer against constant-rate alternatives.

Overview and Commentary

The source addresses a practical rendering problem: modern displays can support high spatial resolution and high refresh rate, but GPUs and transmission links often cannot deliver both at once. Instead of fixing resolution and varying refresh rate, or fixing refresh rate and varying resolution, the paper models which trade-off should look best for the current motion. Its central contribution is a perceptual model that predicts JND-scaled quality differences from refresh rate, image resolution, object velocity, and whether motion is predictable for smooth pursuit eye movement. The model decomposes quality loss into motion-parallel blur, motion-orthogonal blur, and judder. Those components are grounded in visual-system assumptions, equations for hold-type blur, eye-motion blur, resolution blur, and contrast-sensitivity energy, then fitted to psychophysical data. The empirical program is substantial: one experiment measures motion quality from 50 Hz to 165 Hz using pairwise comparisons and active sampling; another measures eye pursuit behavior for predictable and unpredictable motion; a third isolates judder by matching blur across smooth and juddery conditions. The fitted model explains why high refresh rates help but show diminishing perceptual returns above about 100 Hz, why higher velocities demand more temporal resolution, and why unpredictable motion needs separate treatment. The paper then turns the model into MARRR, a motion-adaptive resolution and refresh-rate renderer that uses precomputed lookup tables rather than solving an optimization per frame. Controlled panorama and sandbox game validations show general preference for MARRR over fixed-resolution/fixed-refresh baselines, especially for predictable user-driven motion. The scope is explicit: the work targets high-persistence LCD displays, leaves flicker, ghosting, HDR, low-persistence VR displays, and richer content-aware adaptation for future work, and depends on display synchronization interfaces that were imperfect in the experiments.

Section 02

Model Structure

The model is deliberately interpretable. It predicts quality difference in JND units from refresh rate, resolution, velocity, and predictability, then decomposes the perceptual loss into motion-parallel blur, orthogonal blur, and judder. Hold-type display behavior, imperfect eye pursuit, and resolution reduction are first converted into blur terms; judder is handled through an aliasing and spatio-temporal contrast-sensitivity model.

Section 04

Eye Motion and Predictability

The eye-tracking experiment gives the model a physiological reason to distinguish predictable from unpredictable motion. Above 5 Hz, refresh rate did not materially affect smooth pursuit, but motion predictability did. Unpredictable motion caused more frequent correction saccades and much larger tracking delay, which increases retinal blur and changes the preferred rendering configuration.

Section 05

Judder Calibration

The isolated-judder experiment separates non-smooth motion from blur by artificially matching blur between low-refresh and high-refresh presentations. Observers detected judder at 50 Hz and 60 Hz but not at 72 Hz and 83 Hz. This makes judder a targeted low-refresh component instead of a generic penalty applied equally across the full display range.

Section 06

Fit and Ablation

The ablation study shows why the complete model matters. Parallel blur alone captures some broad trends but misses important velocity differences, while judder alone fits poorly. The best reported fit occurs when parallel blur, judder, and predictable-versus-unpredictable separation are all enabled, indicating that the model's components are complementary.

Section 07

Rendering Application

MARRR applies the model by optimizing refresh rate and resolution under a fixed pixel budget, with lookup tables used for real-time operation. The validation experiments show that observers generally prefer the adaptive result over constant-rate baselines in panorama and game settings, though unpredictable motion and display-control latency expose practical limits in current G-Sync style hardware.

Claims Referencing This Source

  • Blur and judder explain quality

    A motion-quality model can explain refresh-rate and resolution trade-offs by combining motion-parallel blur, motion-orthogonal blur, and judder components in JND units.

  • Predictability affects pursuit accuracy

    Eye-tracking data show that smooth pursuit is not affected by refresh rates above 27.5 Hz but is strongly affected by whether motion is predictable or unpredictable.

  • Velocity lowers perceived quality

    Higher object velocities reduce perceived quality at a fixed refresh rate because they increase motion blur and make content harder to inspect.

  • Judder fades above 72Hz

    Judder is detectable at 50 Hz and 60 Hz but becomes negligible at 72 Hz and higher refresh rates in the isolated-judder experiment.

  • Motion quality is joint

    Perceived motion quality in adaptive rendering depends jointly on spatial resolution, refresh rate, object velocity, and motion predictability rather than on resolution or refresh rate alone.

  • Refresh gains diminish above 100Hz

    Subjective motion-quality measurements from 50 Hz to 165 Hz show that higher refresh rates improve perceived quality, but gains become harder to observe above roughly 100 Hz.

  • Full model fits best

    The full model fits measured motion-quality curves better when it includes parallel blur, judder, and separate handling of predictable and unpredictable motion.

  • MARRR improves adaptive rendering

    The MARRR algorithm uses the perceptual model in real time to choose refresh rate and resolution under a rendering budget, and validation experiments generally prefer it over constant-rate baselines.

  • Taxonomy has three dimensions

    A useful taxonomy for foveated rendering classifies methods by required input data type, foveation principle, and rendering paradigm.

  • Amortization trades variance lag

    Amortized sampling uses a reprojected running average to reduce sampling variance, but the smoothing factor creates a trade-off between noise reduction and temporal lag or blur.

  • Disocclusion requires hole filling

    Disoccluded regions constrain temporal reuse because previous-frame data may be absent; practical systems must reshade these regions or fill holes approximately.

  • Foveation preserves perceived quality

    Foveated rendering can accelerate rendering by giving the foveal region high quality and the peripheral region lower quality while preserving perceived visual quality.

  • Foveation requires three decisions

    Foveated rendering research is organized around three recurring problems: applying human-visual-system perceptual models, rendering regions at different qualities, and integrating foveation into rendering paradigms.

  • Open problems remain broad

    Future foveated-rendering work needs broader use of human visual features, improved gaze and AI techniques, better methods for new data types, more suitable rendering paradigms, stronger objective quality metrics, and deeper AR and task-oriented applications.

  • Perception expands temporal coherence

    Perceptual temporal coherence can enrich rendered content by exploiting visual integration over time, not only by reducing computation through data reuse.

  • Resampling accumulates blur

    Repeated reprojection resampling can attenuate and misplace high frequencies, so temporal reuse needs periodic recomputation, better filtering, or fading of older frames to avoid accumulated blur.

  • Reservoirs amplify candidate counts

    Reservoir sampling makes spatiotemporal reuse practical because a pixel can combine selected samples and weight sums from neighboring reservoirs without storing each candidate stream.

  • ReSTIR reuses light candidates

    ReSTIR samples one-bounce direct lighting by repeatedly resampling light candidates and reusing useful samples across spatial neighbors and prior frames.

  • Coherence amortizes shading work

    Temporal coherence can reduce rendering cost by reusing intermediate or final shading information from previous frames instead of recomputing every frame from scratch.

  • Biased reuse darkens discontinuities

    The biased ReSTIR estimator lowers variance and improves visual quality at interactive render times, but it can lose energy and darken regions near geometric, visibility, or shading discontinuities.

  • Image-space reuse limits scope

    The method depends on useful spatial or temporal correlation and on image-space reservoirs, so performance degrades near disocclusions, fast lighting changes, high geometric complexity, and beyond first-hit direct lighting.

  • HVS models guide foveation

    Visual acuity models and contrast sensitivity models are the two representative perceptual-model families used to guide foveated rendering quality allocation.
