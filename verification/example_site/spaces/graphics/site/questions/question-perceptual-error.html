How should perceptual error be measured in graphics algorithms?

Active question | 2 sources, 9 claims, 9 evidence items, 0 measurements

Current synthesis

Perceptual error in graphics algorithms should be measured against human-visible quality, not only signal error or sample variance. The strongest linked evidence supports JND-scaled psychophysical measurements that decompose error into motion-parallel blur, orthogonal blur, judder, refresh rate, resolution, velocity, and motion predictability, while temporal-reuse algorithms also need explicit checks for lag, accumulated blur, and high-frequency loss.

Conclusions

  • For adaptive rendering, perceptual error is best treated as a JND-scaled quality difference whose inputs include refresh rate, image resolution, object velocity, and motion predictability, with artifact terms for motion-parallel blur, orthogonal blur, and judder. (strong support)
  • Subjective pairwise-comparison experiments provide the most direct calibration target in the linked evidence: quality was measured across 23 refresh rates from 50 Hz to 165 Hz, showing that higher refresh rates improve perceived motion quality but with diminishing observable gains above roughly 100 Hz. (strong support)
  • Motion predictability should be measured or modeled because eye-tracking data show that predictable and unpredictable motion produce materially different pursuit behavior, with unpredictable motion causing more saccades and larger tracking delay despite refresh rates above 27.5 Hz not affecting smooth pursuit in that experiment. (strong support)
  • Judder should be measured as a distinct low-refresh perceptual artifact rather than folded into blur alone, because observers detected isolated judder at 50 Hz and 60 Hz but not at 72 Hz and 83 Hz, and the full model fit measured quality curves better than judder-only variants. (strong support)
  • For temporal accumulation and reprojection-based reuse, perceptual error measurement should include not only variance reduction but also temporal lag, accumulated blur, and high-frequency attenuation or misplacement from repeated resampling. (moderate support)
  • Human temporal integration can make rapid temporal signals appear stable or richer, so perceptual scoring should not assume that every frame-level difference is equally visible, but the linked evidence for this point is qualitative and does not define a concrete metric. (weak support)

Uncertainty

The evidence is strongest for motion-quality modeling on high-persistence LCD displays and for temporal-reuse artifacts. It does not establish one universal perceptual-error metric for all graphics algorithms, scene content, display technologies, HDR, flicker, ghosting, VR low-persistence displays, or content-aware artifacts. Some temporal-coherence evidence is symbolic or qualitative rather than psychophysically validated.
